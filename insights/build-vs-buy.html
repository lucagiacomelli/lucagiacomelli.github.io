OpenAI Is Buying What It Can't Build Fast Enough. Here's What That Means for You.

Six acquisitions in four months. Anthropic choosing compute over M&A. Google paying $2.4B just for a team. The AI giants are making very different bets — and embedded in each decision is a classic MBA framework every company needs right now.

By mid-April 2026, OpenAI had closed six acquisitions. In January alone it announced three in a single month: Convogo, Torch Health, and Crixet. Meanwhile, Anthropic made just three known acquisitions total, instead pouring capital into a 3.5-gigawatt TPU compute partnership with Google and Broadcom. And Google? It paid a reported $2.4 billion — not for a company, but for the CEO and key engineers of Windsurf, in what amounts to the most expensive acqui-hire in recent memory.

Three of the world's most sophisticated AI organisations, each sitting on enormous capital, each facing the same competitive landscape — and each making a fundamentally different choice about how to grow. That divergence is not a coincidence. It's a strategic signal. And it maps almost perfectly onto one of the most durable frameworks in business strategy.

The Framework: Make vs. Buy

The Make-or-Buy decision has been a cornerstone of corporate strategy since Ronald Coase first described transaction costs in 1937, and it was formalised further by Williamson's work on vertical integration. The question is deceptively simple: when is it cheaper, faster, and more strategically sound to build a capability internally, versus acquiring or licensing it externally?

MBA Framework

The Make vs. Buy Decision — Three Dimensions

  • Strategic centrality. Is this capability core to your competitive advantage? If yes, owning it matters more than acquiring it — because you need deep institutional knowledge, not just access.
  • Time-to-market gap. How long would it take to build internally vs. acquire? If the market is moving faster than your build cycle, buying buys you time — but only if integration doesn't erase that advantage.
  • Total cost of ownership. Build cost (engineering, time, opportunity cost) vs. buy cost (acquisition price, integration cost, retention risk, vendor dependency). The sticker price is rarely the real number.

Run each of these dimensions against OpenAI's 2026 acquisition strategy and the logic becomes clear. OpenAI is no longer primarily a research lab — it is assembling a platform company, and platform companies grow through vertical integration. Torch Health gave them a foothold in clinical AI without a multi-year R&D cycle. Convogo brought conversational AI capabilities. Each acquisition is a missing piece in a platform architecture, bought rather than built because the clock is ticking.

"Acquiring a startup provides immediate access to proprietary technology that may take years to develop internally — particularly important in AI where breakthroughs depend on highly specialised expertise."

Why Anthropic Said No to M&A

Anthropic's counter-strategy is equally instructive. Rather than buying capabilities, Anthropic has been buying compute — the raw infrastructure that underpins every AI system. Its 3.5-gigawatt TPU partnership is a bet that the most durable competitive advantage in AI is not software or talent, but access to the physical infrastructure required to train and run frontier models.

Through the Make vs. Buy lens, Anthropic is making a different argument: that its core competency — in the sense Prahalad and Hamel defined it in 1990 — is model quality and safety, not product breadth. Buying a clinical AI startup would dilute focus and introduce integration complexity. Buying compute infrastructure compounds the thing that matters most.

Both strategies can be right. The key insight is that the choice reveals the theory of competitive advantage each company is operating under.

What This Means for Your Company

You almost certainly don't have OpenAI's acquisition budget. But you face the exact same decision structure, at a smaller scale, every time you evaluate an AI capability: do we build it, buy a vendor, or acquire the company?

Applied Framework

How to Run the Decision

  • Step 1 — Define strategic centrality. Ask: if we execute this capability perfectly, does it change our competitive position? If no, buy a commodity vendor. If yes, go to step 2.
  • Step 2 — Assess your time-to-build. Be honest. Factor in hiring, ramp-up, and iteration cycles. If your window of competitive advantage is 12 months and your build cycle is 18, buying is the rational choice.
  • Step 3 — Model total cost of ownership. Include integration cost, key-person retention risk (the talent you're acquiring often walks out within 18 months), and the hidden opportunity cost of engineering bandwidth pulled from your core product.
  • Step 4 — Check your core competency thesis. Anthropic's move only makes sense if you believe compute scarcity is the binding constraint. OpenAI's only makes sense if you believe platform breadth is. What is your binding constraint? Build strategy around that.

The Trap Most Companies Fall Into

The most common mistake I see in AI strategy is buying AI to avoid the hard conversation about what your actual competitive advantage is. A vendor integration feels like progress. It ships fast, it demos well, and it delays the more difficult question of whether you are building something defensible.

OpenAI's acquisitions are strategic because they are assembling a deliberate platform. A company that buys five AI point solutions without a unifying architecture is not executing strategy — it is accumulating technical debt with a good press release.

Key Takeaways
  • OpenAI's M&A blitz and Anthropic's compute bet reflect fundamentally different theories of competitive advantage — both can be right depending on your core competency thesis.
  • The Make vs. Buy framework has three dimensions: strategic centrality, time-to-market gap, and total cost of ownership. Most companies only consider the third.
  • Buying AI capabilities to avoid defining your strategy is the most expensive mistake you can make — the integration cost, retention risk, and opportunity cost compound quickly.
  • Before any AI build/buy decision, define your binding constraint. Is it time? Capital? Talent? Compute? Your answer should determine your approach.
