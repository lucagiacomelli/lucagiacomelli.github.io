Tomorrow morning I'm going under general anaesthetic for ACL reconstruction surgery. Over the past few weeks I've been through three pre-assessment appointments: an orthopaedic surgical consultation, an anaesthesia pre-op review, and a physiotherapy baseline assessment. Between them they generated five clinical letters.

Sitting in the waiting room before my last appointment, I started actually reading them. The surgical consultation letter: patient history collected via intake form, examination findings scored on a standardised rubric, proposed procedure selected from a fixed protocol list, consent confirmation with typed narrative. The anaesthesia letter: ASA risk classification applied to my test results, standard precaution list triggered by risk tier, post-op instructions pulled from the relevant template. The physiotherapy baseline report: range-of-motion measurements, muscle strength test scores, a narrative summary derived directly from those scores.

I read all five. Then I asked myself a question I couldn't quite shake: which of these documents actually required a human to write from scratch?

The orthopaedic surgeon needed to examine my knee. That required a human. The anaesthesiologist needed to review my ECG and make a judgment about risk. That required a human. But the letters that followed those consultations — structured, templated, populated from examination data that had already been recorded — those were a different matter entirely. The clinical judgment had been made. The document was an output of that judgment, not an exercise of it.

That observation sent me looking for what is actually happening with AI in clinical documentation. I expected to find pilots and proofs of concept. What I found was already deployed at scale.

Kaiser Permanente completed the largest generative AI rollout in healthcare history earlier this year, deploying Abridge's ambient documentation system across 40 hospitals and 600+ medical offices in eight states. The technology does one thing: it listens to patient-physician conversations and generates clinical notes automatically. No buttons, no dictation. The physician walks in, sees the patient, walks out — and the note is written.

This is not a glamorous use of large language models. But it is an extraordinarily high-leverage one. At Kaiser's scale — 12,000+ physicians — eliminating even 90 minutes of documentation per day per doctor creates the equivalent of roughly 3,000 additional physician-hours per day. That is the productive capacity of a mid-sized hospital, without hiring a single new person.

And the economics are now well-documented. NVIDIA's 2026 State of AI in Healthcare survey found that organisations investing in AI documentation are averaging $3.20 returned for every dollar invested — with payback in 14 months. That figure is not a projection or a pilot result. It is an average across deployed systems at scale.

"The clinical judgment is the human's job. The letter that follows it doesn't have to be."

Why Documentation Specifically

Of all the places AI could be applied in healthcare, documentation has proven to be the highest-return entry point — and the reasons are structural, not coincidental.

Why It Works

What Makes Clinical Documentation the Ideal AI Target

  • Judgment and documentation are separable. The physician's skill is in the examination and the clinical decision. The letter is an output of that decision — structured, templated, and entirely predictable in form once the inputs are known. Separating those two activities is the insight. AI handles the output; the human handles the judgment.
  • The cost per hour of the person doing it is very high. A physician earning $300,000/year spending 35% of their time on documentation is spending roughly $105,000/year producing compliance paperwork. That is not a rounding error. It is a structural misallocation of the most expensive resource in the building. AI doesn't need to be perfect to be massively better than this arrangement.
  • The task is cognitively demanding but not cognitively creative. Documentation requires precision, domain vocabulary, and awareness of regulatory standards. It is not easy to do well. But it produces no competitive advantage when done by a human rather than an AI. The output has to be accurate; it does not have to be inspired. This is exactly the profile where AI delivers its best returns.
  • Scope is narrow and outcome is measurable. Abridge does one thing. It transcribes and structures clinical conversations into notes. It doesn't attempt to diagnose, recommend, or manage care pathways. That narrowness is not a limitation — it is the source of reliability. When you can define what "done correctly" looks like, you can build a system that does it correctly at scale.

What the Numbers Actually Show

Eleven clinical documents from my pre-assessment appointments. Each one structured, templated, and produced by someone with a decade or more of training — most of whom, by every account, would rather be doing something else.

NVIDIA's 2026 survey found that 63% of healthcare and life sciences professionals are already actively deploying AI — not experimenting, not running controlled trials, deploying. Another 31% are in evaluation. The industry has effectively completed its adoption cycle in two years. That pace doesn't happen because leaders are visionary. It happens because the ROI was large enough and fast enough that the decision became self-evident.

The 14-month payback period is the strategically important number. Most enterprise technology investments operate on three-to-five year horizons. An ERP migration, an infrastructure overhaul — the business case is always a multi-year NPV model with significant uncertainty baked in. A documentation AI that pays back in 14 months behaves more like a hiring decision than a capital investment. A CFO can approve it knowing they will see positive returns before the next annual review. That changes the internal politics of the approval process entirely.

For executives outside healthcare: if you have a high-cost professional population producing structured compliance output as a byproduct of their core work — legal, finance, consulting, engineering — the same logic applies. You don't need to wait for healthcare to finish scaling. The proof of concept is already running across 40 hospitals and 600 clinics.

Key Takeaways
  • Ask whether judgment and documentation are separable in your process. In healthcare, they clearly are. The physician decides; the note follows. Map your own workflows and look for the same pattern: where does expensive human judgment produce a templated, structured output? That gap is your AI opportunity.
  • Measure the cost before you build the solution. Healthcare documentation was measurable — hours per clinician per day, cost per note, error rates. If you don't know your baseline, you cannot build a business case, and you cannot prove ROI after deployment. Spend 30–60 days measuring before spending on tooling.
  • Start narrow and go deep. Abridge does one thing extremely well. The temptation in every organisation is to build something general. The evidence from healthcare is unambiguous: narrow, well-scoped documentation tools outperform broad platforms on every metric. Solve one expensive problem completely, then expand.
  • Use 14 months as your benchmark payback threshold. If your AI documentation case requires more than 18 months to break even, something is wrong — either with the problem selection, the cost model, or the change management plan. The healthcare data is mature enough to serve as a performance baseline. Materially worse projections deserve harder questions before budget is committed.

I'll be back in the operating theatre tomorrow. At some point in the next few days, a surgeon will dictate a procedure note, a physiotherapist will write a post-op baseline report, and an anaesthesiologist will complete a recovery summary. Those documents will take time to produce. Most of that time will not require any of the expertise that justified their salaries. That is the gap that AI now fills — and according to the data, it fills it well.
