Why the Cape Route? Understanding the Normal Path First

To appreciate why Hormuz closing upended global trade, you first need to understand how most of it moves in normal times. A container ship leaving Shanghai or Singapore bound for Rotterdam follows a well-worn corridor: across the Indian Ocean, up into the Gulf of Aden, through the narrow Bab el-Mandeb strait at the mouth of the Red Sea, north through the Red Sea itself, and finally through the Suez Canal into the Mediterranean. Door to door: roughly 11,000 nautical miles and 25–28 days.

That route works because three critical chokepoints — Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb, and Suez — stay open. Remove any one of them and the arithmetic changes completely.

The Chokepoint Cascade

Why Hormuz Closing Also Closes the Red Sea

  • Hormuz closes. The Strait of Hormuz is the exit valve for the Persian Gulf — it controls roughly 21% of global oil flow. When it closed in March 2026, Gulf-origin tankers had nowhere to go.
  • Bab el-Mandeb becomes untenable. The Bab el-Mandeb strait, at the southern tip of the Red Sea between Yemen and Djibouti, is a separate chokepoint — but in a major regional conflict, it doesn't stay neutral for long. Houthi missile activity in 2024–25 had already forced many carriers to avoid it; with full-scale hostilities in 2026, the risk premium on transiting it became uninsurable for most operators.
  • The Red Sea corridor shuts. Even ships that weren't carrying Gulf cargo — container vessels from Singapore, bulk carriers from India — could no longer safely use the Red Sea passage north to Suez. The entire eastern Mediterranean supply chain had to re-route.
  • The Cape becomes the only viable option. With both northern exits blocked or too dangerous, the only open path from Asia to Europe ran south: down the east coast of Africa, around the Cape of Good Hope, north through the Atlantic. A detour of roughly 7,700 extra nautical miles.

In the first week of March 2026, 383 vessels transited the Cape of Good Hope. That number normally hovers around 35. In seventy-two hours after Hormuz closed, the maritime industry executed a rerouting of global trade that would have taken months to model manually. It didn't take months. It happened in hours — because AI systems had already been running the scenarios in the background.

This is not a story about AI replacing ship captains. It is a story about the data infrastructure, routing algorithms, and pricing models now embedded in every major shipping decision — and which were already stress-tested for exactly this tail-risk event.

The Data Backbone: 1.2 Billion Signals a Day

Every commercial vessel above 300 gross tonnes broadcasts its position continuously via the Automatic Identification System (AIS) — a VHF transponder transmitting GPS coordinates, speed, heading, destination, and cargo type every 2–10 seconds. Near coastlines, terrestrial receivers pick up these signals. In open ocean, satellite AIS (S-AIS) takes over.

Kpler operates over 13,000 AIS receivers across 190 countries, tracking more than 350,000 vessels and processing upward of 1.2 billion AIS signals every day. Coastal data latency is under 5 seconds. Open-ocean latency via satellite drops below 60 seconds. Spire Global runs a constellation of 100+ nanosatellites in low Earth orbit, each passing over any ocean region roughly every 90 minutes. exactEarth hosts 58 AIS payloads aboard the Iridium NEXT constellation — meaning coverage is continuous and global.

Raw position data is the input layer. The intelligence is what sits on top of it: weather forecasts, port congestion metrics, fuel price feeds, conflict-zone designations, and historical vessel-performance data, all fused into a continuously updated probabilistic model of every ship's position, next move, and ETA — refreshed every few minutes, for every commercial vessel on earth.

Inside the Routing Algorithm: From Dijkstra to Reinforcement Learning

Maritime route optimisation did not start with AI. For decades, the industry used variants of Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm applied to a waypoint graph — nodes connected by edges weighted by distance, weather risk, and piracy probability. A routing system in 1995 and one in 2015 were not conceptually different. What changed is the real-time input layer and the objective function.

Technical Architecture

The Modern Maritime Routing Stack

  • Layer 1 — Graph construction. The ocean is discretised into a weighted directed graph. Nodes represent waypoints (typically every 0.5° lat/lon in open water, denser near chokepoints). Edge weights encode distance, fuel cost at vessel speed, probabilistic weather delay, conflict-zone risk premium, and destination port congestion.
  • Layer 2 — Dynamic weight updates. An ensemble ML model (gradient-boosted trees + LSTM for time-series) continuously revises edge weights as new AIS, weather, and conflict data arrives. When Hormuz closed, the risk weight on edges passing through the Strait went from ~0.02 to effectively ∞ within minutes of the first closure being confirmed by satellite position data.
  • Layer 3 — Constrained optimisation. A modified A* search finds the minimum-cost path, subject to vessel-specific constraints: draught limits at certain ports, speed curves by engine type, size restrictions in narrow channels. For a VLCC that can't transit Suez anyway due to dimensions, the Cape route is the only mathematically valid path regardless of the blockade.
  • Layer 4 — Fleet-level RL coordination. Individual vessel optimisation creates collective inefficiency — every ship routing to the same alternative port simultaneously causes congestion not captured in the model. Reinforcement learning agents learn policies that optimise across the fleet: staggering arrivals, splitting cargo, and dynamically repricing departure windows to avoid self-inflicted bottlenecks.

The key insight from the algorithmic layer is speed of adaptation. Platforms like Windward had modelled Hormuz-closure scenarios before February 2026. When the signal arrived, it was not a novel computation — it was a pre-built contingency that got activated. The industry had been stress-testing this scenario for years.

When Routes Change, Prices Move Instantly — and AI Is Trading Both

The Cape of Good Hope route from the Persian Gulf to Rotterdam covers approximately 14,500 nautical miles. The Suez route (when open) covers roughly 6,800 nautical miles. That gap — 7,700 extra nautical miles — flows immediately into freight economics.

For a VLCC running at 14 knots, the Cape route adds approximately 23 extra days at sea. At 80 tonnes of bunker fuel per day and $650/tonne, that is $1.2 million in additional fuel cost per voyage before a single dollar of charter rate is considered. Charter rates for VLCCs spiked to $80,000–$120,000/day during the crisis — a level not seen since the COVID demand shock. The pricing cascade ran simultaneously across spot freight rates, Forward Freight Agreements (FFAs), tanker futures, and oil options — and algorithmic trading systems executed the arbitrage faster than any human desk could respond.

What This Means for Any Business That Touches Global Supply Chains

The Hormuz crisis is, in a narrow sense, a shipping story. In a broader sense, it is a demonstration of what happens when real-time AI is embedded at every layer of a physical supply chain — and what happens to companies that don't have that capability when the model changes overnight.

Business Framework

Three Tiers of Supply Chain AI Readiness

  • Tier 1 — Real-time visibility. You know where your cargo is, updated every few minutes, with probabilistic ETAs. This is now table stakes. Companies still relying on weekly shipping updates from brokers are operating with a 5-day information lag in a market that moves in hours.
  • Tier 2 — Scenario modelling. Your procurement team can run the Hormuz-closes scenario in your digital twin before it happens, with pre-negotiated contingency contracts and rerouting playbooks ready to activate. Windward and Kpler both offer this layer. Most mid-market companies don't use it.
  • Tier 3 — Algorithmic response. Your system automatically reroutes, adjusts purchase orders, reprices customer commitments, and triggers hedges — without waiting for a human to open a spreadsheet. This is where the frontier sits. It is achievable. Most companies are not there.

The companies that handled the Hormuz closure best were not the ones with the biggest procurement teams. They were the ones that had invested in live data infrastructure before the crisis — meaning when the signal arrived, the model updated and the decision was half-made before a single email was sent.

Key Takeaways
  • AIS satellite networks now track 350,000+ vessels with sub-60-second open-ocean latency — the raw data infrastructure for real-time supply chain AI already exists and is commercially accessible via Kpler, Spire, and Windward.
  • Modern maritime routing stacks layer A* graph search with dynamically ML-updated edge weights and fleet-level RL coordination. When Hormuz closed, risk weights updated in minutes, not weeks.
  • The Cape of Good Hope route adds ~23 days and over $1.2M in fuel alone for a VLCC — this is the real-time calculation that algorithmic freight pricing systems now execute continuously across spot rates, FFAs, and options.
  • Supply chain AI readiness has three tiers: visibility, scenario modelling, and algorithmic response. Most companies are at Tier 1. The competitive advantage in the next disruption sits at Tier 3.
