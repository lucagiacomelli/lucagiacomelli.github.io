LLM API prices have dropped roughly 80% between 2024 and 2026, declining at 30–50% per year consistently. Today you can run GPT-5 nano at $0.05 per million input tokens, or opt for a frontier model like Claude Opus 4.6 at $5/$25 input/output. The price compression has been relentless, driven by hardware improvements, competition, and the commoditisation of inference infrastructure.

For companies building AI-powered products, the instinct is to celebrate. Lower input costs mean higher margins, right? Sometimes. But the relationship between LLM costs, gross margin, and competitive positioning is more complicated — and most founders I speak to haven't modelled it properly.

The Unit Economics Framework for LLM Products

In any software business, gross margin is the foundation. It tells you how much of each revenue dollar remains after paying for the direct costs of delivering the service. For traditional SaaS, those costs are low — hosting and infrastructure rarely exceed 10–15% of revenue. Gross margins of 70–80% are standard.

LLM-powered products break this model. Every user interaction has a variable cost tied directly to usage: tokens in, tokens out, model selected. As your product scales, your COGS scales with it — and unlike a server cost that amortises across users, each additional query costs real money.

MBA Framework — Unit Economics

The Key Metrics for LLM Products

  • LLM Cost per User (monthly): Avg queries/day × avg tokens/query × days × token price. This is your LLM-driven COGS per user.
  • LLM as % of Revenue: If your LLM cost per user exceeds ~20% of ARPU, you have a structural margin problem as you scale.
  • Gross Margin: Revenue − (LLM costs + infrastructure + support). Best-in-class LLM products target 60–70%. Below 50% is a warning sign.
  • Break-even ARPU: The minimum you can charge per user before LLM costs alone make the unit unprofitable. This sets your pricing floor.

The Commoditisation Paradox

Here is the strategic trap embedded in falling LLM prices. When inference gets cheaper, two things happen simultaneously. Your costs go down — good. But so does the cost for any competitor to replicate your product — bad. Lower inference costs reduce barriers to entry.

If your moat is "we use GPT-5 to do X," that moat is already gone. Any well-funded competitor can run the same model, at the same cost, tomorrow. The companies that will win on unit economics are those building advantages that don't scale linearly with inference costs: proprietary data, fine-tuned models on unique datasets, deep workflow integration that creates switching costs, and network effects.

"Cheaper inference is a commodity. What you build on top of it — your data, your workflow, your compounding advantages — is what determines whether the margin flows to you or to your competitors."

The Three Strategic Responses

When LLM costs fall, every AI product company faces a strategic choice about what to do with the savings:

1. Pass savings to customers (compete on price). This makes sense if you're in a commoditised market where price is the primary differentiator and you have scale advantages. Dangerous if competitors can match your pricing — you race to zero.

2. Retain savings (expand margin). Rational if customers aren't price-sensitive and your product has strong retention. The risk: a competitor enters at lower price and steals your users before you can defend.

3. Re-invest savings in product quality. Use the cost reduction to run better models, more queries, or richer features at the same price point — increasing your quality gap without touching margins. This is the most strategically sound approach if you're building for defensibility.

What to Do With This Model

The calculator above is a simplified view, but it surfaces the critical variables fast. If your LLM costs are already above 30% of revenue at current prices, you have a margin problem that cheaper models won't solve — you have a pricing or usage problem. If your costs are sub-10%, you have strategic room to either expand your model usage, improve product quality, or defend against price competition.

The most important number to watch is not the absolute cost, but the trajectory. Model LLM costs as a percentage of revenue at 2×, 5×, and 10× your current MAU. If that percentage rises as you scale, you don't have a scalable business — you have a cost structure that gets worse with success.

Key Takeaways
  • LLM API prices fell ~80% from 2024 to 2026 — but falling COGS also reduces barriers to entry for competitors, compressing your pricing power.
  • LLM-powered products must track cost-per-user, not just total cost — and model how that number scales with usage growth.
  • A sustainable AI product targets LLM costs below 20% of revenue. Above 30% is a structural warning sign.
  • The strategic response to falling inference costs is not to pass all savings on — it's to reinvest in the product advantages that can't be commoditised: proprietary data, fine-tuning, and workflow depth.
